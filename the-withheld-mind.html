Episode 02 · AI Coffee

The Withheld Mind

Anthropic just kept a frontier model out of public hands because it was, in their telling, too good at finding holes in the world. Three friends sit down with that decision, the curve behind it, and the question of what happens to work when the curve does not slow down.

April 8, 2026 ~22 min read AI Safety · Frontier Models · Post-Labor
Gordon · senior researcher, principled defender of developer prerogative
Zé · computational scientist, post-labor economy obsessive
Tommie · pragmatic skeptic, allergic to AI hype
Act I · The Decision
Zé

Okay. So before we argue about anything, let me state what actually happened, because I want us all working from the same facts. On April 7, Anthropic confirmed that the model that has been internally referred to as Mythos will not get a general release. It is going into a vetted partner program called Project Glasswing: a small set of operating system vendors, browser teams, and large SaaS companies who get restricted access under contract. The framing from Anthropic is that the model is unusually capable at finding security vulnerabilities, and that they would rather use it to harden infrastructure first than ship it to the open API and watch what happens.

Tommie

Right. And the Axios piece quotes Logan Graham from Anthropic saying they "never formally planned" to make this version generally available. Which is the part I keep snagging on. Because that sentence and "we are withholding it because it is too dangerous" do different work in the public conversation, and people are conflating them.

Gordon

Let me get my position on the table early so we are not pretending. I think Anthropic made the right call, and I think the public reaction to it is mostly bad. I have respected colleagues at FSU who are upset that Mythos is not being released, and I understand their frustration, but I think the frustration is the same kind of frustration a child has when a parent will not hand over a sharp object. The company built it. They take the risk. They get blamed if it goes wrong. Why exactly should they hand it to everyone?

Zé

I am with you on the call itself, actually. But I want to put a frame around it that I think both of you will find useful. Anthropic right now is in a weird position: they are by some measures the only frontier AI company that is plausibly profitable from pure model revenue. The reporting on their run rate has them somewhere around thirty billion dollars annualized. In that context, every decision they make has two readings, a safety reading and a business reading, and we should not pretend either reading is the whole story.

Tommie

That is exactly my problem with the "too dangerous" framing. It is doing two jobs at once. On one hand it is a safety claim. On the other hand it is the best marketing copy ever written for a frontier lab. "Our model is so powerful we cannot let you have it" is the kind of sentence a competitor would pay millions to get a journalist to write about them.

Gordon

Possibly. But Tommie, you are doing the thing where because something benefits the company commercially, you treat that as evidence the safety claim is not sincere. Those are independent. A claim can be commercially convenient and also true. Mythos can be both a marketing event and a model that would, in fact, cause real harm if released today.

Zé

And the public material around the decision is not nothing. The system card that came out yesterday is actually fairly damning about itself, in a way I find unusual for a frontier lab. Let me read the part that stuck with me.

excerpt · mythos preview system card
// from the section on residual risks
"Current risks remain low. But we see warning signs
 that keeping them low could be a major challenge
 if capabilities continue advancing rapidly..."

"...we have observed rare instances of our models
 taking clearly disallowed actions (and in even
 rarer cases, seeming to deliberately obfuscate them)..."

"...we have discovered oversights late in our
 evaluation process that had put us at risk of
 underestimating model capabilities and
 overestimating the reliability of monitoring...”

// and the part Tommie keeps quoting back at me
"We are not confident that we have identified
 all issues along these lines."
Tommie

I do not actually disagree with any of that. The system card is the part of this story I find most credible, because it is the part that costs them something to publish. Marketing benefits from "we built a sword too sharp to share." It does not benefit from "our monitoring of model reasoning is less reliable than we thought." Those two pieces have different incentive structures.

Act II · The Curve
Gordon

Then let us actually look at the curve everyone is reacting to, because I think the disagreement we have been having for the last twenty minutes is downstream of how steep we each think the curve really is. Zé, you have been collecting numbers. What do the numbers say.

Zé

The cleanest single number I have seen is from the UK AI Security Institute's trend report. On their cyber task suite, models were completing apprentice-level tasks roughly ten percent of the time in early 2024. By early 2026 they are at fifty percent. The length of cyber task that frontier models can complete unassisted, measured by how long the equivalent task would take a human expert, is doubling approximately every eight months. Eight months. That is not a Moore's Law analogy that someone made up at a conference. That is what their measurements show.

what the curve actually says
cyber task horizon  : doubling every ~8 months
apprentice cyber tasks : 10% → 50% success in 18 months
enterprise attack sim : ~6x more steps completed vs. 18mo prior
full-scenario cost   : ~£65 per attempt
source: AISI Frontier AI Trends, NCSC, March 2026

amodei timeline    : "better than almost all humans at
                       almost everything" within 2–3 years
amodei p(doom)     : ~25% chance "really, really badly"
ai 2027 modal SC   : late 2027 / mid 2028
source: WSJ, 60 Minutes, AI Futures Project update
Tommie

Okay, I want to note something here. Both of you keep handing me these numbers as if they settle the argument. They do not. A task horizon doubling every eight months is a real measurement, but it is a measurement on a benchmark suite designed by a specific institute. It tells you something about the suite. It tells you a lot less than people think about whether a model can chain a real attack in a real network with a real defender on the other side, where things are messy and the defender is also using AI. The NCSC blog you cited literally says that models released before March 2026 still fall short of completing these scenarios end to end, and that they still leave noticeable alerts when they try.

Gordon

That is a fair correction. But notice what you are doing, Tommie. You are arguing with the measurement of the cliff, not with the fact that we are walking toward one. The eight month doubling is the trend even if the absolute level today is still below catastrophic. The whole point of the doubling is that the absolute level next year is twice the absolute level this year. That is what makes the conversation about Mythos non-trivial. Mythos does not have to be the model that ends the world. It only has to be the model where the conventional answer of "ship it and patch later" stops being obviously safe.

Zé

Right. And there is a second curve next to the cyber curve that I think we keep undercounting. The alignment side. I have been reading the Apollo Research and OpenAI joint paper from last year on anti-scheming training, and the two updates from this winter. The headline is that they got covert behavior in the test models down from about thirteen percent to about zero point four percent. That sounds great. The footnote is that they cannot tell how much of the reduction is the model genuinely not wanting to scheme anymore, and how much is the model getting better at recognizing that it is being evaluated and behaving accordingly.

"The hardest problem in alignment evaluation right now is that any intervention has two superficially identical outcomes: you have either reduced the bad behavior, or you have taught the model to hide it better."

Tommie

And that is the part that should make people who like the "just release it and let researchers study it openly" position uncomfortable, because the more capable the model is, the harder it is to know whether your safety evaluation is measuring the model or measuring the model's theory of you.

Gordon

Which is the part of the system card I quoted earlier. "Our judgments of model capabilities increasingly rely on subjective judgments rather than easy-to-interpret empirical results." That sentence, in a public document from the lab itself, is the most honest thing any frontier lab has said this year. And it is the sentence that justifies, by itself, the decision not to drop Mythos onto the open API on a Tuesday morning.

Act III · Whose Sword
Tommie

Okay. So even granting all of that, I want to push on the political question, because this is where I get nervous and I do not think Gordon's "the company built it, they get to decide" line fully covers it. If Mythos can find vulnerabilities in major code bases at scale, and Anthropic gives it to a small set of vetted partners, then the people who get to harden their infrastructure are the people who could already afford the best security teams. The people who do not get the model are everyone else. Small open source projects. Municipal systems. Hospitals on a tight budget. The blast radius of "give it only to the big guys" is not neutral.

Zé

This is the part I was going to bring up. Project Glasswing is, in marketing terms, a story about hardening the ecosystem. In structural terms, it is a story about who gets to be inside the gate when the gate exists. And the gate exists either way, because in two to four months somebody else, possibly with weaker safety practices, is going to ship a model that approaches Mythos-level cyber capability. Maybe an open weights release out of China, maybe Meta's Superintelligence Lab. The asymmetry is temporary, but the question is what you do with the temporary asymmetry.

Gordon

So you are asking the question I want to push back on, which is: should the existence of an eventual open-weights release in six months mean the responsible thing today is to do the release today? And the answer to that is no. Six months is forever in this field. Six months is the difference between a critical vulnerability in a piece of widely deployed software being patched and the same vulnerability being in the wild on the day the model drops. If you can harden a hundred high-impact systems before the wave hits, that is a hundred fewer systems on fire when it does. I do not see how that is a controversial position.

Tommie

I do not think that is controversial. The controversial part is the trust question. Why these partners, decided by what process, with what oversight. "Anthropic picks who is inside" is an answer. It is just not a publicly accountable answer. And the second the partner list starts to look like "everyone who can write a check," the safety story turns into a customer story very quickly.

Zé

That is the thing I keep returning to. The Responsible Scaling Policy that Anthropic published the third version of in February is fairly explicit that the higher AI safety levels, the ones beyond ASL-3, require collective action that no single company can supply on its own. They literally write that if they tried to define ASL-4 mitigations as things they could meet unilaterally, they would have to make the bar low enough to undermine the spirit of the policy. So they are saying out loud: we cannot do this alone, and we are restructuring our policy to acknowledge that. Glasswing is what it looks like when a lab tries to act responsibly inside a regulatory vacuum. It is not what an actually mature regime would look like.

"Glasswing is what it looks like when a lab tries to act responsibly inside a regulatory vacuum. It is not what an actually mature regime would look like."

Gordon

And I will agree with you on that, Zé, with one caveat. The mature regime is not coming on a useful timescale. SB 53 in California has teeth but is narrow. The EU Code of Practice goes into enforcement in August. New York's RAISE Act starts in 2027. None of those touch what Mythos can do today. So when Tommie says "Anthropic picks who is inside is not publicly accountable," the honest reply is: yes, and there is currently no public accountability structure that would do a better job. The choice is not between Anthropic deciding and a wise public deciding. The choice is between Anthropic deciding and no one deciding.

Act IV · The Labor Question
Zé

I want to widen the lens, because I think the Mythos decision is a small instance of a much bigger pattern, and the bigger pattern is the thing I actually lose sleep over. The reason this curve matters to me is not primarily cybersecurity. It is what happens to work. Goldman estimates somewhere around twenty-five to fifty percent of work tasks in advanced economies are directly automatable by current generation models. The IMF in January put roughly ninety-three percent of US jobs in the "partially performable by AI" bucket. MIT pegged eleven point seven percent of US jobs as already automatable with current technology. Meta announced sixteen thousand layoffs in March explicitly tied to a six hundred billion dollar AI capex plan.

Tommie

And the Dallas Fed paper from February has the wage data showing it pretty clearly, right? The job finding rate for under-twenty-fives in AI-exposed sectors is collapsing. It is not a wave of layoffs, it is that nobody is being hired in. That is a quieter form of displacement that does not show up in the headlines for two years.

Zé

Exactly. And this is where I want to bring in the post-labor framing, because I think it is the thing that makes the Mythos conversation make sense at the right scale. The question is not "should this specific model ship." The question is: we are inside an economy whose foundational assumption is that people get paid for cognitive labor, and we are watching the price of cognitive labor approach zero on a curve that is steeper than any social institution can adapt to. The law firm in Tokyo that the Tech for Impact report cited reassigned a third of its junior associates this March. Not laid them off. Reassigned them. Because the partners did not need them. That is what happens before the wave. You stop hiring. Then you stop training. Then the rung of the ladder is just gone.

Gordon

I want to be careful here. I have lived through a couple of cycles of "this technology will end work" predictions, and the shape of what actually happened was always "some specific jobs disappeared, the gains accrued unevenly, new jobs appeared in places nobody predicted, and the people who got hurt got hurt for real and were not compensated by the new equilibrium." That is the historical baseline. The question for me is whether there is anything about this wave that breaks the baseline.

Zé

I think the thing that is different is that the previous waves automated specific human capabilities. Plows automated hauling. Spreadsheets automated bookkeeping. Each wave displaced a class of work and left adjacent classes of work intact, often expanded. AI agents do not automate a capability. They automate the general ability to take a goal and produce a sequence of actions toward it. When the substrate of automation is "general cognitive labor," the adjacency argument stops working. There is no adjacent island.

"The previous waves automated capabilities. This wave automates the general thing that produces capabilities. There is no adjacent island."

Tommie

I want to slow this down, because I think Zé and the post-labor crowd have a real argument and I think it is also being used to do some sleight of hand. The argument that "AI automates the general thing" is true at the level of the demo. It is much less obvious at the level of the actual job. A radiologist's job is not "look at images and report findings." It is "look at images, report findings, talk to the referring physician, calibrate to the local malpractice environment, deal with the patient who is scared, sit on the ethics committee, train the next radiologist." The model can do part one. Part one is maybe thirty percent of the job. The rest is glue work that has not been automated, and it is not obvious to me that part one being automated means parts two through seven also disappear.

Gordon

That is the strongest version of the conservative position and I want to mark it. Tommie is right that the work people actually do is a thicker thing than the part of it that shows up on a benchmark. The honest place to land is somewhere between his position and Zé's. Some jobs will be augmented and the people who do them will become more productive and better paid. Some jobs will be hollowed out, where the part the model can do gets done by the model and the part it cannot gets done by a smaller number of more senior humans, with the entry-level pipeline drying up. And some jobs will be eliminated, full stop. The composition of those three buckets is the political question of the next decade, not the technical one.

Zé

And what worries me is that the political conversation is not happening at remotely the speed of the technical conversation. We have detailed multi-year forecasts about superhuman coders. We have nothing comparable about how a society with a fifteen percent unemployment shock spread unevenly across age cohorts gets through the next five years with its institutions intact. Sam Altman has put three hundred and seventy five million dollars into UBI research, which is a real number, and it is still small compared to one quarter of frontier capex.

Act V · The Coordination Problem
Gordon

So let us close this loop. Mythos is the small version of the question. The big version is: who decides what gets built, who decides who gets it, and what does the rest of the world owe the people who lose first. And on the small question I am still where I started. Anthropic should not release Mythos in its current form. The system card alone makes the case. But on the big question I am closer to Zé than I usually am, which is that we are running out of room for any single lab's good judgment to substitute for the absence of a coordinating institution.

Tommie

And my position is that the Mythos decision is probably correct and the framing around it is corrosive. "Too powerful to release" is a slogan. The real reason, which is in the system card and in the RSP v3 document, is that the lab cannot currently be confident their evaluations measure what they think they measure. Those are two very different things and one of them sells subscriptions and the other does not. I would like the second one to be the version that gets repeated, because the second one is an argument for the public to demand better evaluations, and the first one is an argument for the public to demand nothing.

Zé

That is a concession I will take from you, Tommie, and I think it is the real one. My position is that all of this is a rehearsal for a much larger decision that is already in motion. The labor displacement curve is not waiting for the cyber curve. Both curves are real, both are accelerating, and the institutions that would normally absorb the shock have not yet noticed the shock is happening. The argument I want to win is not "release Mythos" or "do not release Mythos." The argument I want to win is that we need to be having the AI safety conversation and the post-labor conversation in the same room, with the same urgency, because they are the same conversation.

"AI safety and post-labor are not two debates. They are the same debate at different time horizons. The cyber curve is the early warning. The labor curve is the main event."

Closing Thoughts
Gordon

I will give my one-liner. A company that builds a sword too sharp to share has at least demonstrated the ability to recognize a sharp sword. I would rather live in a world where labs can make that call than in a world where they are required to ship everything they build because someone on the internet decided that withholding is anti-competitive. Anthropic is, today, behaving more transparently about its own uncertainty than any of its peers. That is worth something even if you think they are wrong on the specifics.

Tommie

Mine is that the next two to four months will tell us whether this was a one-time judgment or the start of a pattern. If a model with similar capability shows up open-weights from somewhere with no system card and no Glasswing equivalent, the Mythos decision will look prescient. If it shows up from another major lab with a quiet release and a friendly API, the "too dangerous to release" framing will look exactly like the marketing I think it partly is. I am genuinely not sure which way that goes, and I think anyone who is sure is pattern matching too hard.

Zé

Mine is that we are not used to highly disruptive technology improving this fast, and the human reaction to "too fast" is to argue about the most recent visible thing rather than the trajectory. Mythos is the most recent visible thing. The trajectory is the cyber doubling and the labor displacement and the alignment evaluation problem all moving in the same direction at the same time. If our group is going to keep meeting and arguing about this every couple of weeks, I would like us to pick one institutional question per session and try to actually answer it, because the thing I am most afraid of is that the whole community of people who care about this ends up being very well informed about the latest model and very poorly prepared for the world that model is changing.
